\section{Spatial graph topology}
\label{sec:part1}

The spatial $k$-nearest-neighbour graph is the substrate of every analysis that follows: niche detection in \partref{2} operates on it, the GNN in \partref{3} consumes it through neighbour sampling, and the per-class and per-niche networks in Parts \ref{sec:part4}--\ref{sec:part5} are downstream of the niches it induces. The first question, therefore, is what this graph captures across the 112 samples of the cohort, and to what extent its topology reflects biology rather than artefacts of tissue handling or segmentation.

I summarise each sample with a free-tier of eight cheap-to-compute metrics --- mean degree, fraction of isolated nodes, fraction of cells in the largest connected component, number of components of size $\geq 100$, global transitivity, mean local clustering, degree assortativity, and cell-type assortativity --- and an expensive tier of vertex-level statistics (closeness, betweenness) computed on a subset of ten samples. The free tier is fast enough to run on the full cohort: the resulting parquet table has 112 rows and 22 columns and is loaded once at the start of every downstream notebook. Two samples (\#87 and \#107) emerge as topological anomalies during the free-tier pass and are excluded from subsequent cohort-level analysis, leaving 110 well-behaved samples.

\subsection*{Local topology is consistent across the cohort}

The first observation is that the local statistics of the graph vary very little across patients. Figure~\ref{fig:part1_mean_degree} shows the distribution of mean degree per sample. After excluding the two anomalies, 105 of 110 samples concentrate between mean degree 6 and 9, with a median of 8.24 and a tight, single-peaked shape; the four samples below 6 bottom out at 5.10. This is essentially what one would expect from a KNN construction with $k = 8$ followed by symmetrisation and a radius cap: every sample sits close to that ceiling, with deviation driven by how often the radius cap discards far-away neighbours in sparse regions. Local transitivity is similarly uniform across the cohort (mean $\approx 0.53$, low spread), and the mean local clustering coefficient varies in a narrow band.

\begin{figure}[h]

    \includegraphics[width=0.78\linewidth]{part1_mean_degree_distribution.pdf}
    \caption{Per-sample mean degree of the spatial graph, across the 112 samples of the cohort. The two anomalies (\#87, \#107) sit far in the left tail at mean degree below 4 and are excluded from cohort-level analyses. The remaining 110 samples concentrate tightly around the median (8.24).}
    \label{fig:part1_mean_degree}
\end{figure}

\subsection*{Global connectivity varies over the full range}

Local consistency does not extend to global connectivity. The fraction of cells belonging to the single largest connected component (LCF) --- a global statistic about how the tissue holds together --- spans almost the entire achievable range across the cohort, from $0.11$ to $0.999$ (Figure~\ref{fig:part1_bimodality}). The distribution is strongly left-skewed with a mode near $1.0$: 37 samples have $\mathrm{LCF} \geq 0.90$ and are essentially a single contiguous piece of tissue, what I call \emph{compact architecture}; at the other end, 19 samples fall in the $[0.25, 0.45]$ range, with their cells split across many roughly equal components in tendril-like fragments, which I call \emph{fragmented architecture}. The two labels are convenient endpoints, not distinct populations: the intervening range is the most densely occupied part of the tail (50 of the 110 samples have $\mathrm{LCF}$ between $0.45$ and $0.90$), and a Hartigan dip test gives no evidence against unimodality ($\text{dip} = 0.025$, $p = 0.93$).

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.78\linewidth]{part1_lcf_bimodality.pdf}
    \caption{Histogram of the largest connected component fraction (LCF) across 110 samples (anomalies excluded). The distribution is left-skewed with a single mode near $1.0$ and a long tail reaching $0.11$; a Hartigan dip test finds no evidence of bimodality ($p = 0.93$). The \emph{compact} ($\mathrm{LCF} \geq 0.90$, $n = 37$) and \emph{fragmented} ($\mathrm{LCF} \in [0.25, 0.45]$, $n = 19$) labels used throughout the report are coarse summaries of the two ends of a continuum, not distinct architectures.}
    \label{fig:part1_bimodality}
\end{figure}

This is an important finding for downstream work: many of the analyses in Parts \ref{sec:part2}--\ref{sec:part5} subsample 5000 cells from a tissue region, and they will behave differently along the connectivity gradient. I revisit this point in \partref{2}, where niche assignment recovers a median of 96\% of cells in compact samples but only $\sim 32\%$ in fragmented ones --- a near-mechanical consequence of low LCF, since niche detection runs on the giant component and cells outside it cannot be assigned. Coverage in fact tracks the LCF almost exactly across the whole cohort (Spearman $\rho = 0.994$), which is why the gradient, rather than any two-class split, is the quantity that matters downstream.

I also checked whether the architecture is patient-consistent --- whether the two samples of a patient share a similar LCF. They do not: across the 54 patients with both samples in the working cohort, the intraclass correlation of the LCF is $\mathrm{ICC}(1,1) = 0.016$, so essentially none of the variance in tissue connectivity is attributable to the donor. (I use the ICC rather than a Pearson correlation between paired samples, since the two samples of a patient have no canonical ordering.) Tissue architecture is therefore a per-sample property, not a per-donor one. This rules out the simplest interpretation (some patients have "compact tissue" by biological default) and points toward sample-level handling factors as the more plausible source of fragmentation.

\subsection*{Topology correlates with cell-type composition}

Topology statistics are not just artefacts of the graph construction; they correlate with what is in the sample. Figure~\ref{fig:part1_composition_topology} shows the Spearman correlation between the eight topology metrics and the per-sample fraction of each cell-type class, computed across the 110 non-anomaly samples.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{part1_composition_topology_heatmap.pdf}
    \caption{Spearman correlation between eight topology metrics (rows) and the per-sample fraction of each cell-type class (columns), across the 110 non-anomaly samples. SMC (\classid{5}) dominates the structure: it strongly anti-correlates with local density ($\rho = -0.58$ vs.\ mean degree) and positively correlates with fragmentation indices ($\rho = +0.51$ vs.\ isolated fraction, $\rho = +0.64$ vs.\ number of components $\geq 100$, $\rho = +0.62$ vs.\ degree assortativity). T cells (\classid{6}) carry the strongest negative cell-type assortativity signal ($\rho = -0.38$), consistent with their boundary-crossing infiltrating role.}
    \label{fig:part1_composition_topology}
\end{figure}

Two features of this heatmap are worth highlighting. First, SMC (\classid{5}) stands out as the dominant driver of cohort-level sparsity. Samples rich in SMC have lower mean degree ($\rho = -0.58$), more isolated cells ($\rho = +0.51$), more components of size $\geq 100$ ($\rho = +0.64$), and stronger degree assortativity ($\rho = +0.62$). In other words, SMC marks the sparse, fragmented, self-coherent regions of tissue --- consistent with the perivascular and connective localisation of smooth muscle cells, which occupy lower-density spatial niches than the dense epithelial or stromal compartments.

Second, T cells (\classid{6}) show a distinctive pattern: they negatively correlate with mean local clustering ($\rho = -0.50$) and, more tellingly, with cell-type assortativity ($\rho = -0.38$, the strongest negative value for this metric among the eight classes). The latter means T cells tend to sit in regions where their neighbours are predominantly of \emph{other} cell types --- a quantitative signature of their infiltrating, boundary-crossing behaviour. This is the first cross-Part signal of a finding that will recur: T cells in tumour tissue are diffuse populations that occupy heterogeneous spatial contexts rather than homogeneous neighbourhoods. \partref{2} will confirm that they never dominate any niche, \partref{3} that they are the worst-classified cell type, and \partref{4} that their gene network has no overlap with any other class.
\subsection*{The training mask reflects local density}

The dataset provides a binary \texttt{train\_mask} that indicates which cells were used for model training in the original benchmarking. Figure~\ref{fig:part1_mask_vs_degree} shows that the per-sample fraction of cells retained by this mask is essentially a function of local density: Spearman $\rho = -0.91$ with mean degree. Dense samples have less of their cells in the training mask; sparse samples have more. This is a strong constraint to keep in mind for \partref{3}, where any model trained on the mask is implicitly trained on a density-biased subset of the cohort.

\subsection*{Betweenness reveals tissue skeletons}

The cheap-tier metrics are sample-level scalars. To probe the per-cell structure I computed betweenness centrality on the giant connected component of ten representative samples, using Brandes--Pich source/target sampling \cite{brandes2007centrality} (full betweenness on a 42{,}000-node graph is computationally prohibitive). The result is unexpectedly visual: in every sample, the top 1\% of cells by betweenness form a connected, branching pattern that traces the structural backbone of the tissue. Figure~\ref{fig:part1_betweenness} shows sample 2, where 425 cells of the 42{,}423-cell giant component are highlighted; the same pattern reproduces qualitatively on the other expensive-tier samples.

I checked whether betweenness-high cells are enriched for any particular cell-type class. Across the expensive-tier samples, no consistent enrichment emerges: the skeleton picks up whichever cell type happens to populate the inter-block bridges in that sample. The structural role is graph-defined, not biology-defined. Closeness centrality, in contrast, behaves essentially as an inverse Euclidean distance from the centroid of the sample ($r = 0.81$); it adds little information beyond geometry, and I drop it from subsequent analyses.

\subsection*{Anomaly handling}

Two samples (\#87 and \#107) trip the anomaly check during the free-tier pass, defined as mean degree $< 5$ and largest-component fraction $< 0.2$: sample \#87 has mean degree $3.4$ and $\mathrm{LCF} = 0.02$, sample \#107 mean degree $1.6$ and $\mathrm{LCF} = 0.05$. Figure~\ref{fig:part1_anomaly_handling} compares one of them, sample \#87, against a representative normal sample (\#2): at the same absolute spatial scale, the anomalous sample displays a cell population that is both sparser and distributed over a much larger spatial extent. Quantitatively, sample \#2 covers a $22\,000 \times 26\,000$-unit region with $43{,}023$ cells and a median nearest-neighbour distance of $52$ units; sample \#87 covers a $54\,000 \times 60\,000$-unit region with only $24{,}268$ cells and a median nearest-neighbour distance of $92$ units. The 99th percentile of nearest-neighbour distance is even more telling: $107$ units for sample \#2 against $455$ for sample \#87. Because the spatial graph applies a cohort-wide radius cap calibrated on the typical inter-cell distance, the KNN edges in sample \#87 are systematically rejected by the cap: cells exist in the data, but they are too far apart to form a graph that resembles a connected tissue. The single small giant component visible at the bottom right of the panel is the only cluster dense enough to survive the cap.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.0\linewidth]{part1_anomaly_handling.png}
    \caption{Anomaly handling: comparison of a representative normal sample (\#2) against the two anomalous samples (\#87, \#107) at a common spatial scale ($30\,000 \times 30\,000$ units, centred on each sample's centroid). Cells are coloured by cell type if they belong to the giant connected component of the spatial graph, light grey otherwise. The scalebar in each panel marks $5{,}000$ units. Sample \#2 fills its panel with densely connected coloured cells; samples \#87 and \#107 show cells distributed at a much lower density over a much larger spatial extent, with only a small giant component (the few coloured cells) surviving the radius cap on KNN edges.}

I interpret these as tissue-handling or segmentation failures (necrosis, sample disgregation, or segmentation that recovered only a subset of cells) rather than as a biologically meaningful sparse architecture; either way, the KNN-with-radius-cap construction is not a suitable substrate for their analysis. Both samples are excluded from all cohort-level analysis from this point forward. The remaining 110 samples form the working cohort used in Parts \ref{sec:part2}--\ref{sec:part5}.

\medskip

\noindent\textbf{Summary.} The spatial graph is locally uniform across the cohort but globally heterogeneous: the largest-component fraction spans a continuous gradient from $0.11$ to $0.999$, with compact and fragmented tissue as its two ends rather than as distinct architectures. Topology correlates with cell-type composition, with SMC as the dominant driver of sparsity. The training mask is almost entirely a function of local density. Per-cell betweenness identifies a tissue skeleton with no cell-type signature: it is a graph property of the geometry, not of the biology. These observations inform every downstream analysis, and constrain how robustly the niche detection in \partref{2} and the GNN in \partref{3} are expected to generalise across the connectivity range of the cohort.
